U.S. low-cost carriers (LCCs) arrive 15+ minutes late more often than full-service carriers (FSCs). We asked whether that gap widens in peak season, and moved from monthly totals to 5.16 million individual flights to compare like with like.

H1γ > 0

The gap is larger in peak months. One-sided permutation test over 126 labellings, α = 0.05, set before running.

G1Research question

Is the LCC–FSC delay gap larger in peak season?

Comparing LCC and FSC flights that depart from the same airport, on the same day, in the same hour, is the gap in the probability of a 15+ minute arrival delay larger in peak season than in off-peak season?

Delayed = β·LCC + γ·(LCC × Peak) + same-cell + controls
βLCC − FSC gap off-peak
β + γLCC − FSC gap in peak
γHow much more the gap grows in peak. This is the answer.
Sub-question 1 · Mechanism

If the gap grows, does it move together with shorter scheduled ground time between an LCC aircraft's flights? Answer: no (G6).

Sub-question 2 · Condition

Do we get the same answer if "peak" means a busy day, a crowded hour or severe weather? Answer: a small widening on busy days and crowded hours, none with severe weather (G4, G6).

How we defined peak season

Main definition · months

Peak = the three months of 2024 with the most scheduled flights per day, counted across all carriers reporting to BTS, including regional airlines: June, July, August. Off-peak = the three with the fewest: January, February, December. The six middle months are left out so the two groups are clearly different.

Scheduled flights per day, all carriers reporting to BTS (cancelled flights included). Daily averages are used because February has 29 days.

How we got here

Our first definition used calendar months (Jul, Dec vs Jan, Apr) and showed no effect (γ = , p = ). Traffic data showed December is among the quietest months, so we redefined peak season as the three months with the most scheduled flights. We also report a daily, airport-level Peak Index as a secondary analysis.

Secondary definition · busy days Peak Index = departures today ÷ that airport's usual same weekday − 1

+0.10 means the departure airport is 10% busier than its usual day of the same weekday. Counted across all carriers reporting to BTS and compared with the same weekday, so the weekly pattern is removed and the study carriers' own schedules carry less weight.

iInformation · Limits

What we can and cannot claim

  • LCC status is a business model, not a switch. We report an adjusted gap, not the effect of "becoming" an LCC.
  • Only nine carriers, so ordinary standard errors overstate certainty. We rely on the permutation test over all 126 ways to label five of the nine carriers as LCC.
  • The baseline gap β is not clearly separable from carrier differences (permutation p ≈ 0.06–0.11): Delta and United are far better than American and Alaska, which look like LCCs.
  • The peak definition changed once (calendar months to traffic-ranked months); both versions show no widening.
  • The busy-day effect is small (about 0.8 pp on a day 15% busier than usual) and appears only when flights are compared within the same cell; DML without that matching does not confirm it.
  • Shorter ground time is real for LCCs but does not explain the gap; ground time is also a scheduling choice, so this is association, not proof.
  • Flight counts measure planned traffic, not passengers.
